My Automated Blog Went 99 Days Without a Post. Nothing Broke.

Written by hybs02

build logfailure logautomationClaude Code

If your automated blog has stopped publishing and the scheduler says every run succeeded, check the queue before you check the code. Mine was empty.

The first three posts on this site went up by hand on July 25, 2026. As I write this on November 1, nothing new has gone up since: 99 days. I went looking for the break and there wasn't one. The site was up, the pages loaded, and the publishing scripts were where I'd left them.

Nothing failed. The queue was empty.

The setup behind this site can publish on its own. Finished posts wait in a queue folder, and a scheduled task on my PC takes the next one on Mondays and Thursdays and runs the whole chain: build, push, deploy.

On this site, the queue was empty and I had never switched the scheduled task on. So for 99 days nothing ran, and nothing logged an error.

Another blog I run, on the same pipeline, has been quiet since early August: 90 days as of today. There the scheduler did run. On most Monday and Thursday mornings it woke up, found nothing to publish, wrote BACKLOG_EMPTY to its log and exited with success. I'd designed an empty queue to count as a normal ending rather than an error, so nothing ever turned red.

I run the two sites as a pair, and when I stopped writing for one, I stopped for both.

If your scheduled blog has gone quiet

These are the checks that would have told me in a minute:

  • Open the publish log, not just the site. On the other blog it said BACKLOG_EMPTY twice a week for three months.
  • In Windows Task Scheduler, find the task and read Last Run Time and Last Run Result. A result of 0 means the job ran and exited normally. It doesn't mean anything was published.
  • Count the files in the queue folder. Zero files and a successful task is exactly the state I was in.
  • If there's no task at all, the site was never automated in the first place. That was this one.

The step I never automated

Writing new posts was always meant to be a human step. When I built the pipeline, Claude Code's cloud scheduler couldn't reach my setup, and I couldn't get it to run unattended on the PC either. I also wanted to read every post before it went out. So the plan was simple: when the queue gets low, I ask Claude Code to write more, I read the drafts, and the approved ones go in the queue.

I did build a warning: a small control panel for both sites that flags a queue running low or empty. It only warns me while it's open, and I wasn't opening it.

I'd learned this once already. That same pipeline once sat dead for over a month because a launcher file had the wrong line endings and I never read the log. A warning you have to go and look at isn't much of a warning, and knowing that didn't stop it from happening again.

Refilling is now a Claude Code skill

This time I didn't just ask for more posts. I asked Claude Code to turn the whole refill job into a skill. In Claude Code, a skill is a written procedure plus a few small scripts, kept together, which it follows whenever I ask for that kind of job. This one covers both sites.

Health checkDays since the last post, posts in the queue, whether the scheduled task exists, whether the live site responds
TopicCandidates come from things I actually built or broke on my PC, and I pick

If you want to try the same thing, this is close to what I'd ask for if I were starting over:

Turn my blog refill job into a skill. Start with a health check: days since the last post, how many reviewed posts are in scheduled/, whether the scheduled task exists, and whether the live site responds. Then suggest topics from things I actually did on this PC, ask me for any number or error text you can't find in my files, draft to match my existing posts, run the word-count and phrase checks, have a fresh instance review the draft without our conversation, and stop for my approval before anything goes into the queue.

The first health check on this site came back with three problems: nothing in the queue, 99 days since the last post, and no scheduled task registered at all.

The machine checks for this site are tuned to its own posts, not to a generic rule. The three July posts ran from about 1,250 to 1,500 words, so a draft under 1,200 gets flagged. They used 5 to 8 dashes per thousand words, so more than 8 gets flagged, because heavy dash use reads as machine-written to a lot of readers. And the word "honest" turned up in all three. That's a tic, and the check now counts it.

The reviewer that didn't know the history

The step that paid for itself was the blind review. It's the same Claude, started fresh, without any of the conversation that produced the draft. It gets three things: where the file is, who the reader is, and what they'd type into a search box. I don't tell it what I was going for or what I think of the result, because when I do, the critique tends to agree with me.

I tested it first on the post I point every new reader to: the build log for this site. It came back with "not publishable."

The post said this site costs about $12 a year for its domain. This site doesn't have its own domain. It runs on a free github.io address. The post said "ten posts in" and quoted roughly 880 visitors a month. Those were the other blog's numbers, and this site had three posts. It listed an email signup this site has never had, and it described the scheduler publishing posts here, which had never happened once.

When that post was drafted in July, the other blog's facts came along with the story, and I didn't catch it. For three months, the page I send new readers to first described a different website.

I rewrote it and ran the reviewer again, this time pointing it at my deploy script, my setup notes and the repository's Pages settings. It found that the setup steps I'd just added told readers to serve the site from the root folder, while this site serves from a /docs folder. That one never went live.

None of these were caught by the machine checks. Word counts and phrase lists can't tell you that a number belongs to the wrong website. A reader with no memory of writing the post, checking it against the files, could.

What's different now, and what isn't

One more post is queued behind this one: the tool I built to sell digital products, and why it still lists zero of them. Posts go out on Mondays and Thursdays, one at a time.

The skill drafted two more, a GPU speed test and a video encoder comparison, and I held them back. They were accurate, but they were about the tools, not about the blog or about earning anything, which is what this site is for.

The part I haven't solved is the one that caused this. The skill checks everything the moment I ask, and nothing at all when I don't. If I stop asking, both sites stop again, and the scheduled task will keep reporting success.

I don't have a good fix for that yet. The obvious one is an alert that comes to me, an email or a phone notification when the queue reaches zero, instead of a panel I have to open. I haven't built it. Until I do, the health check is the first thing the skill runs, and the next status post goes up whether the numbers look good or not.

This site carries no ads and has earned nothing so far, so the 99 days didn't cost money. On a site that did earn, the same silence would have had a price, and the scheduler would have reported success the whole time.
