I Generated 8 Books in One Day. Listing Them Was the Hard Part.

Written by hybs02

KDPautomationfailure log

On May 31, 2026, I used Claude Code to generate eight English Kindle books in one day. Around 30,000 words each. Roughly a quarter of a million words, produced between waking up and going to bed, with a workflow I'd spent a while refining.

As of November 13, seven of the eight are on sale as Kindle ebooks and one isn't listed yet. The first went on sale in early June and sold no copies in the 30 days before November 1. The other six went up from the end of October on.

Most posts about AI books stop at the first part. This one is about the gap between "made" and "on sale."

What that day actually looked like

I wasn't typing prompts for sixteen hours. I'd built a repeatable process by then: pick the niche and the angle it would be differentiated on, generate an outline, draft chapter by chapter, run quality passes over the result, produce a cover, convert to ebook format. Because the tool works on real files and runs real commands, that whole chain runs mostly unattended. I checked in, redirected when something drifted, and let it work.

By the end of the day: eight folders, eight complete manuscripts, eight covers, eight sets of metadata. Marginal cost close to nothing beyond a subscription I was already paying for.

I remember the feeling clearly. It felt like I'd built a factory.

Then five months went by

Here's what I hadn't understood. Everything I automated was the part that was already cheap.

Getting a book onto the store is not a computational problem. It's a sequence of small human tasks in a web interface, per title, and none of them are hard. That's exactly what makes them so easy to keep not doing.

For each book: title and subtitle, a description written to sell rather than to explain, keyword slots, category choices, pricing decisions, a cover file that has to satisfy actual spec, a preview pass to confirm nothing renders wrong on a device, and a disclosure of AI-generated content, which the platform requires and which stays required no matter how heavily you edit afterward. Then there's account-level setup, tax details and banking, which is one-time but blocks everything until it's done.

There's also a cap now. Since late September 2026, KDP has limited new ebook titles to two a week, counted separately for creating and for submitting. When I generated these books in May the limit was much higher, so the cap isn't what held them up through the summer. It's what sets the pace for the rest.

One of the eight went on sale in early June. The other seven sat in a folder on my laptop through June, July, August, September and most of October.

The one that went live without me noticing

The strange part is that I didn't know the first one was live.

I keep a tracking file for the books: one line each, with a state like draft or live. For five months it said all eight were drafts. In late October I had Claude Code read the actual bookshelf page in my KDP account and compare it against the file, and one of the "drafts" had been on sale since early June, with an older cover and an older ebook file than the versions I'd since fixed. My records don't show how it got from draft to submitted. It has since been replaced with the fixed version.

For five months I counted from my own file, not the store. The store was right. The file was wrong.

That comparison is now a step in the routine: the shelf is checked against the file before anything else gets decided, and the file is corrected to match the shelf.

Getting the rest up

Two more went up at the end of October. Claude Code filled in most of the listing forms in the browser, using the metadata it had generated with each manuscript. One box it couldn't tick: KDP asks you to confirm that your answers about AI-generated content are true, and that confirmation has to come from me. Asking for both books at once meant one request and two ticks, in one sitting. Two more went up on November 5, and two more on November 8 and 12, which is as fast as the weekly cap allows. How the listing runs now, and the one box that's still mine, is in the post about Claude Code listing the books.

Before listing anything, the eight books got a check that turned up problems I would have shipped otherwise. Two covers carried a different title from the one in the listing, which the store would have rejected. One ebook file had part of its content broken by formatting. All eight repeated their chapter titles twice. Each fix took minutes once it was found. Finding them took a full pass over all eight.

The check I didn't count

The real reason for the five months is one I dodged for a while.

Every book has to be checked before it goes on sale.

A quarter of a million words. That's the part I generated in a day and could not possibly read in a day. Even skimming with intent (does this chapter deliver what the heading promised, is this claim true, does the whole thing say the same thing three times in different clothes) takes days of concentrated attention. In May I had left that check to myself, and it's boring enough that I kept not doing it.

Shipping them unchecked is the shortcut, and people take it. The store's own guidelines prohibit content that disappoints buyers, and thin output gets removed, sometimes along with the account. Unchecked AI books in bulk are also exactly what's flooding these stores. Something has to decide each book is fit to sell.

That decision isn't made by me now. I don't read the books. Each one goes through machine checks (word count, readability, phrases that read as AI, wording that sounds like a made-up anecdote, repetition), then a blind read: a separate Claude instance that knows nothing about how the book was made reads it cover to cover as a buyer, and reports where it lost interest and what looked padded or invented. A book that passes is listed and published by Claude Code. That check isn't airtight. A later review found inconsistencies and errors in books already on sale, and on November 12 all seven got corrected files.

So the accounting is this. Generating took care of the writing, not the checking, and the checking was the step I hadn't planned for. Writing used to be the wall. Once it was gone, the wall was checking, listing, and knowing what's actually on the shelf.

What the queue actually is

I used to look at that folder and see eight assets.

I've stopped. Unpublished manuscripts are not an asset. They generate nothing, they can't be tested against a real market, and they're aging. The niche moves, the competition ships, and the angle I picked in May is less sharp in November.

There's also a compounding problem I walked straight into. Because generating is so cheap, the temptation when publishing stalls is to generate more. It feels productive, and it produces a visible number that goes up. It's the opposite of progress: it lengthens the queue at the step that isn't the constraint.

I was measuring "manuscripts generated." The number that matters is "books on sale," and for most of this year I had that number wrong too, because I was counting from my own records instead of the store.

What I should have done, in order

Not advice. Just the plan I'd give my May self.

Publish one book end to end before generating a second. All the way to live, timing every step, including the check. That single run tells you the real cost per book, and the real cost is the only input that matters when you decide whether to scale. I didn't time any of it, so I still don't have that number.

Build the automation around the slow step. I optimized generation because it's the fun part and the part a tool handles well. The right target was everything downstream: metadata produced with each manuscript, the listing form filled in by the tool, and the check before sale done by machine checks and a blind reader instead of waiting for my free weekend. All of that runs now. None of it did in May.

Count what's on sale, and count it at the store, not in your own notes. A tracking file that you update by hand will drift from the store. Mine said zero for five months while the answer was one.

Treat "I can do it later" as the actual risk. A hard task gets scheduled. A task that's merely tedious gets deferred forever, because there's never a moment when doing it beats not doing it. Five months of evidence on that one.

What I proved is narrower than what I set out to prove, but it's real: the writing constraint is gone. That used to be the entire wall.

Removing the biggest constraint doesn't leave you with no constraint, though. It promotes the next one, and the next one turned out to be a web form, a weekly cap, my own attention, and a tracking file that disagreed with the store. Three orders is too few to say anything about. When there's a month of royalties to show, the numbers will be here, good or embarrassing.
